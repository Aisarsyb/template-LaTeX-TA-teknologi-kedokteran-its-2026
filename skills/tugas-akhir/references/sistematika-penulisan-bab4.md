# Chapter 4 guide: research results and discussion

Use when drafting or reviewing Chapter 4. Official program guidelines and supervisor instructions determine the final structure; the outline below is not mandatory.

## What makes Chapter 4 effective

- **Systematic:** Subsections follow the research objectives, variables, testing stages, or themes; readers can find results for each objective.
- **Objective:** Figures and quotations match their sources; explanations stay within the evidence, especially when distinguishing technical device performance from clinical benefit.
- **Detailed but concise:** Each important finding has evidence and context; details that do not help answer the objectives belong in an appendix.
- **Clear visuals and consistent presentation:** Use tables or figures when they clarify findings. Explain them in the text and use consistent terms, units, and numbering.

## Before writing

- Check the Chapter 4 and Chapter 5 titles in the thesis, the objectives or research questions in Chapter 1, the methods and variable definitions in Chapter 3, and the available results. Determine whether discussion belongs in Chapter 4 or a separate chapter. This step is complete when each objective has relevant data and the location of its discussion is clear; flag missing data or unresolved decisions.
- Organize results by research objective, variable, testing stage, or theme actually supported by the data. Include a description of the study object or setting and sample or participant characteristics only when they help interpret results; an opening paragraph and a results summary are also optional. This step is complete when readers can trace each objective to its results.

## Presenting results

- Report what was found, including unexpected results. Check every number, unit, sample size, label, and statement against the source data. Describe missing data or unusual findings without inventing causes. For health-related or medical-device results, distinguish measured performance from untested clinical benefit.
- **Quantitative:** Choose descriptive statistics appropriate to the data (counts and percentages; mean and standard deviation; or median and spread). If inferential tests were performed, report the test, result or effect size, uncertainty such as a confidence interval when available, and *p* value and degrees of freedom when relevant. Describe associations as associations; causal claims require a study design that supports them.
- **Qualitative:** Organize findings by theme, supported by quotations, observations, or documents with enough context. Explain what the evidence shows and how themes relate. Select representative quotations rather than copying entire transcripts. Protect participants' identities and check permission to use photos or quotations that could identify them.
- **Tables and figures:** Use tables for precise numbers or multiple attributes; charts for patterns, comparisons, distributions, or trends; flow diagrams for processes; and photos for relevant visual evidence. Provide numbers and titles, labels and units, explanations of abbreviations, sources for third-party data, and legends where needed. Use honest scales and ensure readability, including in black-and-white print. Introduce each visual in the text, then explain its key findings without repeating all its contents. Choose the most useful format rather than duplicating the same data in a table and chart without reason.

## Results narrative

- Open each section with the objective or finding it addresses. Introduce a table, figure, or quotation before showing it; afterward, highlight one or two important findings and explain how they relate to the research objective rather than repeating every number.
- Describe comparisons, trends, and unusual results using their magnitude and the limits of the available data. Use consistent terms and units; call a statistical result “significant” only when a test supports that claim. Explain direct findings in Chapter 4; place explanations of possible causes and comparisons with theory wherever the thesis places the discussion.
- Write scientific paragraphs linking findings to evidence and explaining their relevance to the health problem studied. This step is complete when readers can identify the main finding, its data source, and the limits of its interpretation without guessing what a table or figure means.

## Placing the discussion

- **If discussion is in Chapter 5:** Chapter 4 presents results and explanations directly supported by the data. Discuss comparisons with theory or previous studies, possible explanations, implications, and limitations in Chapter 5. Refer to variable or instrument definitions from earlier chapters when needed to understand the results, without repeating the methods.
- **If discussion is part of Chapter 4:** Clearly distinguish results from discussion, whether organized by objective or theme. After presenting results, explain why findings matter, compare them with relevant literature including conflicting findings, and discuss evidence-supported explanations, limitations, and clinical implications within the study's scope. Keep final conclusions and recommendations in the chapter specified by the thesis structure.

## Final checks

- Ensure every research objective is answered by results or explicitly identified as unanswered; every claim must rest on verifiable data or citations. Recheck numbers in the text, tables, and figures against their sources, and protect participant anonymity.
- Check transitions between subsections, spelling, terminology, numbering, cross-references, and visual readability in the PDF. Follow the LaTeX class and applicable guidelines rather than setting fonts, margins, or numbering manually in this guide. The review is complete when no known discrepancies remain; report sections still requiring data or a supervisor decision.
